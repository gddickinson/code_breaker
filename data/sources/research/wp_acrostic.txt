An '''acrostic''' is a [[poetry|poem]] or other word composition in which the ''first'' letter (or syllable, or word) of each new line (or [[paragraph]], or other recurring feature in the text) spells out a word, message or the alphabet.<ref name="acrostic">{{Cite web |title=Acrostic Poetry |url=http://outstandingwriting.com/acrostic-the-many-pleasures-of-acrostic-poetry/ |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161010233051/http://outstandingwriting.com/acrostic-the-many-pleasures-of-acrostic-poetry/ |archive-date=2016-10-10 |access-date=2011-04-30 |publisher=OutstandingWriting.com}}</ref> The term comes from the French {{Lang|fr|acrostiche}} from post-classical [[Latin]] {{Lang|la|acrostichis}}, from [[Koine Greek]] {{Lang|grc|ἀκροστιχίς}}, from [[Ancient Greek]] {{Lang|grc|ἄκρος}} "highest, topmost" and {{Lang|grc|στίχος}} "verse".<ref>{{Cite encyclopedia |title=s.v. ''acrostic'' |encyclopedia=[[Oxford English Dictionary]] |edition=3rd |quote=The expected spelling of the English word, on the n., ''monostich'' n.}}</ref> As a form of [[constrained writing]], an acrostic can be used as a [[mnemonic]] device to aid memory retrieval.  When the ''last'' letter of each new line (or other recurring feature) forms a word it is called a '''telestich''' (or '''telestic'''); the combination of an acrostic and a telestich in the same composition is called a '''double acrostic''' (e.g. the first-century Latin [[Sator Square]]).

Acrostics are common in medieval literature, where they usually serve to highlight the name of the poet or his patron, or to make a prayer to a saint. They are most frequent in verse works but can also appear in prose. The Middle High German poet [[Rudolf von Ems]] for example opens all his great works with an acrostic of his name, and his world chronicle marks the beginning of each age with an acrostic of the key figure (Moses, David, etc.). In chronicles, acrostics are common in German and English but rare in other languages.<ref>{{emc1||Acrostics|8-10}}</ref>

== Form ==
Relatively simple acrostics may merely spell out the letters of the alphabet in order; such an acrostic may be called an 'alphabetical acrostic' or [[abecedarius]]. These acrostics occur in the [[Hebrew Bible]] in the first four of the five chapters of the [[Book of Lamentations]], in the praise of the good wife in [[Proverbs 31]][[Proverbs 31#The Good Wife (31:10–31)|:10-31]], and in [[Psalm]]s 9-10, [[Psalm 25|25]], [[Psalm 34|34]], [[Psalm 37|37]], [[Psalm 111|111]], [[Psalm 112|112]], [[Psalm 119|119]] and [[Psalm 145|145]].<ref name="biblicalhebrew">{{Cite web |title=Acrostic Psalms |url=http://www.biblicalhebrew.com/alphabet.htm |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080917055036/http://www.biblicalhebrew.com/alphabet.htm |archive-date=17 September 2008 |access-date=2008-09-09 |website=biblicalhebrew.com}}</ref>
Notable among the acrostic Psalms is the long [[Psalm 119]], which typically is printed in subsections named after the 22 letters of the [[Hebrew alphabet]], each section consisting of 8 verses, each of which begins with the same letter of the alphabet and the entire psalm consisting of 22 x 8 = 176 verses; and [[Ashrei|Psalm 145]], which is recited three times a day in the [[Jewish services]]. Some acrostic psalms are technically imperfect. For example, [[Psalm 9]] and [[Psalm 10]] appear to constitute a single acrostic psalm together, but the length assigned to each letter is unequal and five of the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet are not represented and the sequence of two letters is reversed. In Psalm 25 one Hebrew letter is not represented, the following letter ({{Lang|he|Resh}}) repeated. In Psalm 34 the current final verse, 23, does fit verse 22 in content, but adds an additional line to the poem. In Psalms 37 and 111 the numbering of verses and the division into lines are interfering with each other; as a result in Psalm 37, for the letters {{Lang|he|Daleth}} and {{Lang|he|Kaph}} there is only one verse, and the letter {{Lang|he|Ayin}} is not represented. Psalm 111 and 112 have 22 lines, but 10 verses. Psalm 145 does not represent the letter {{Lang|he|Nun}}, having 21 one verses, but one Qumran manuscript of this Psalm does have that missing line, which agrees with the [[Septuagint]].  Some,  like O Palmer Robertson,  see the acrostic Psalms of book 1 and book 5 of Psalms as teaching and memory devices as well as transitions between subjects in the structure of the Psalms.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Robertson |first=O. Palmer |title=The Flow of the Psalms |date=2015 |publisher=P&R Publishing |isbn=978-1-62995-133-1 |pages=80–81}}</ref>

Often the ease of detectability of an acrostic can depend on the intention of its creator. In some cases an author may desire an acrostic to have a better chance of being perceived by an observant reader, such as the acrostic contained in the ''[[Hypnerotomachia Poliphili]]'' (where the key capital letters are decorated with ornate embellishments). However, acrostics may also be used as a form of [[steganography]], where the author seeks to conceal the message rather than proclaim it. This might be achieved by making the key letters uniform in appearance with the surrounding text, or by aligning the words in such a way that the relationship between the key letters is less obvious. These are referred to as ''[[null cipher]]s'' in steganography, using the first letter of each word to form a hidden message in an otherwise innocuous text.<ref name="Garykessler">{{Cite web |title=Steganography |url=http://www.garykessler.net/library/steganography.html |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080908083126/http://www.garykessler.net/library/steganography.html |archive-date=8 September 2008 |access-date=2008-09-09 |publisher=Garykessler.net}}</ref> Using letters to hide a message, as in acrostic ciphers, was popular during the [[Renaissance]], and could employ various methods of enciphering, such as selecting other letters than initials based on a repeating pattern ([[equidistant letter sequences]]), or even concealing the message by starting at the end of the text and working backwards.<ref name="cryptology">{{Cite web |title=Cryptology |url=http://home.att.net/~tleary/cryptolo.htm |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080917142151/http://home.att.net/~tleary/cryptolo.htm |archive-date=17 September 2008 |access-date=2008-09-09}}</ref>

According to [[Cicero]], acrostics were a regular feature of [[Sibylline Books|Sibylline prophecies]] (which were written in Greek [[hexameter]]s. The type of acrostic is that known as a “gamma acrostic” (from the shape of the Greek letter {{lang|grc|Γ}}), where the same words are found both horizontally and vertically.<ref>{{lang|la|de Divinatione}} (2.111–112). The passage is quoted and translated in {{harvnb|Hejduk|2018|pp=75–76}}</ref> Cicero refers to an acrostic in this passage using the Greek word {{lang|grc|ἀκροστιχίς}}.

The 3rd-century BC didactic poet [[Aratus]], who was much admired and imitated by Cicero, Virgil and other Latin writers, appears to have started a fashion for using acrostics. One example is the famous passage in ''Phaenomena'' 783–7 where the word {{lang|grc|λεπτή}} {{gloss|slender, subtle}} occurs as a gamma acrostic and also twice in the text, as well as diagonally in the text and even cryptically taking the initial letters of certain words in lines 2 and 1:<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Danielewicz |first=J. |year=2015 |title=One sign after another: the fifth λεπτή in Aratus ''Phaen.'' 783–4? |url=http://journals.cambridge.org/abstract_S0009838814000780 |journal=The Classical Quarterly |volume=65 |issue=1 |pages=387–390|doi=10.1017/S0009838814000780 |url-access=subscription }}</ref>

===Latin===
Several acrostics have recently been discovered in Roman poets, especially in [[Virgil]]. Among others, in [[Eclogue 9|''Eclogue'' 9]] the acrostic {{lang|la|VNDIS}} {{gloss|in the waves}} (lines 34–38) immediately precedes the words {{lang|la|quis est nam ludus in undis?}} {{gloss|for what is your game in the waves?'}}, and {{lang|la|DEA DIO}} (i.e. {{lang|la|dea Dione}} {{gloss|the goddess Dione}}) (lines 46–51) in a passage which mentions the goddess [[Dione (Titaness)|Dione]] (another name for [[Venus (mythology)|Venus]]).<ref name="Adk">{{harvnb|Adkin|2014|pp=45–46}}, citing {{harvnb|Grishin|2008}}</ref> In [[Eclogue 8|''Eclogue'' 8]], alongside a passage dedicating the poem to an unnamed person and asking him to accept it, Neil Adkin reads the words {{lang|la|TV SI ES ACI}} (i.e. {{lang|la|accipe}}) ({{gloss|if you are the one, accept!}}).<ref name="Adk" />

In ''Aeneid'' 7.601–4, a passage which mentions [[Mars (mythology)|Mars]] and war, describing the custom of opening the gates of the [[Temple of Janus (Roman Forum)|Temple of Janus]], the name {{lang|la|MARS}} (the god of war) appears in acrostic form as well as in the text as follows:<ref>Pointed out by {{Cite journal |last=Fowler |first=D. P. |year=1983 |title=An Acrostic in Vergil (Aeneid 7.601-604)? |journal=Classical Quarterly |volume=33 |page=298|doi=10.1017/S0009838800034522 }}</ref>

:{{lang|la|'''M'''os erat Hesperio in Latio, quem protinus urbes}}
:{{lang|la|'''A'''lbanae coluere sacrum, nunc maxima rerum}}
:{{lang|la|'''R'''oma colit, cum prima movent in proelia '''Martem''',}}
:{{lang|la|'''S'''ive Getis inferre manu lacrimabile bellum&nbsp;…}}

:“It was a custom in [[Hesperia (mythology)|Hesperia]]n [[Latium]], which originally the [[Alba Longa|Alban]]
:cities kept as sacred, and now, greatest of entities,
:Rome keeps, whenever they move [[Mars (mythology)|Mars]] to the first battles,
:whether (they are preparing) to bring tearful war to the [[Getae]]&nbsp;…”

In ''Georgics'' 1 429–433, next to a passage which contains the words {{lang|la|namque is certissimus auctor}} {{gloss|for he is the most certain author}}, the double-letter reverse acrostic ''MA VE PV'' (i.e. Publius Vergilius Maro) is found on alternate lines.<ref name="Adk" />

In [[Eclogue 6]], 13–24 Virgil uses a double acrostic, with the same word {{lang|la|LAESIS}} {{gloss|for those who have been harmed}} going both upwards and downwards starting from the same letter L in line 19.<ref>Discovered by {{harvnb|Adkin|2014|p=46}}</ref> Another double acrostic is found in ''Aeneid 2'', where the word ''PITHI'' (i.e. {{lang|grc|πείθει}}, Greek for he ‘persuades’ or ‘he deceives’) is found first backwards at 103–107, then forwards at 142–146, at the beginning and end of a speech by Sinon persuading the Trojans to bring the wooden horse into the city.{{sfn|Adkin|2014|p=57}} The discoverer of this acrostic, Neil Adkin, points out that the same word {{lang|grc|πείθει}} occurs at more or less exactly the same line-numbers in a repeated line describing how Odysseus’ wife Penelope deceived the suitors in ''[[Odyssey]]'' 2.106 and 24.141.

Another transliterated Greek word used as an acrostic in a pseudo-Sibylline prophecy has recently been noticed in the syllables ''DE CA TE'' (i.e. Greek {{lang|grc|δεκάτη}} {{gloss|tenth}}) in [[Eclogue 4]], 9–11, with the same ''DEC A TE'' repeated cryptically both forwards and backwards in line 11.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Kronenberg |first=Leah |date=2017 |title=The Tenth of Age of Apollo and a New Acrostic in ''Eclogue'' 4 |url=https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/phil-2016-0120/html |journal=Philologus |volume=161 |issue=2 |pages=337–339 |doi=10.1515/phil-2016-0120|url-access=subscription }}</ref>

In another pseudo-Sibylline prophecy in poem 5 of [[Tibullus book 2]] the words {{lang|la|AVDI ME}} ‘hear me!’ are picked out in the first letters of alternate lines at the beginning of the prophecy.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Kronenberg |first=L. |year=2018 |title=Tibullus the Elegiac Vates: Acrostics in Tibullus 2.5 |journal=Mnemosyne |volume=71 |issue=3 |pages=508–514 |doi=10.1163/1568525X-12342338 |jstor=26572934}}</ref>

Virgil’s friend [[Horace]] also made occasional use of acrostics, but apparently much less than Virgil. F. (Ed.). (2011). ''Horace: Odes IV and Carmen Saeculare''. Cambridge University Press.</ref> In the first poem of Horace's ''Epodes'' (which were also known as {{lang|la|Iambi}} 'iambics'), the first two lines begin {{lang|la|ibis ... amice}}, and it has been suggested that these words were deliberately chosen so that their initial letters IBI ... AM could be rearranged to read IAMBI.<ref>Evans, B. (2023). "Τwo Beginnings: Acrostic Commencements in Horace (Epod. 1.1–2) and Ovid (Met. 1.1–3)". ''The Classical Quarterly'', 73(2), 699-713.</ref> In his famous lines in the {{lang|la|Ars Poetica}}, lines 136–9, containing the proverb "mountains will give birth, but there will be born (nothing but) a ridiculous mouse", Horace writes a telestic MVS joined to the word {{lang|la|mus}} 'mouse', adding to the humour of the passage. The 3rd century AD poet [[Commodian]] wrote a series of 80 short poems on Christian themes called {{lang|la|Instructiones}}. Each of these is fully acrostic (with the exception of poem 60, where the initial letters are in alphabetical order), starting with {{lang|la|PRAEFATIO}} ‘preface’ and {{lang|la|INDIGNATIO DEI}} ‘the wrath of God’. The initials of poem 80, read backwards, give {{lang|la|COMMODIANUS MENDICUS CHRISTI}} ‘Commodian, Christ’s beggar’.

== Multiple acrostics ==

===Double acrostics===
[[File:Sator Square at Oppède.jpg|thumb|right|A Sator square (in SATOR-form), on a wall in the medieval fortress town of [[Oppède|Oppède-le-Vieux]], France]]
A '''double acrostic''', may have words at the beginning and end of its lines, as in this example, on the name of [[Stroud, Gloucestershire|Stroud]], by Paul Hansford:
  '''S''' et among hills in the midst of  five valley '''S''',
  '''T''' his peaceful little   market town we inhabi '''T'''
  '''R''' efuses  (vociferously!) to  be  a  conforme '''R'''.
  '''O''' nce home  of  the cloth  it gave its name t '''O''',
  '''U''' phill and down again its  streets  lead  yo '''U'''.
  '''D''' espite its faults it leaves  us all  charme '''D'''.

The first letters make up the acrostic and the last letters the telestich; in this case they are identical.

Another example of a double acrostic is the first-century Latin [[Sator Square]].<ref name="EB">{{Cite web |title=Sator square |url=https://www.britannica.com/topic/SATOR-square |access-date=17 September 2022 |website=[[Encyclopedia Britannica]]}}</ref>

===Complex acrostics===
[[image:Triple Acrostic by Thomas Browne.jpg|right|200px]]
The poem ''Behold, O God!'', by [[William Browne (poet)|William Browne]],<ref name="WilliamBrowne">{{Cite web |title=William Browne: Behold O God! |url=https://www.presscom.co.uk/leepriory/leebrowne.html |access-date=2009-05-15 |website=Presscom.co.uk}}</ref> can be considered a complex kind of acrostic. 
In a document held in the [[Church of St Michael, Withington]], Gloucestershire, some letters are capitalized and written extra-large, non-italic, and in red, and the lines are shifted left or right and internally spaced out as necessary to position the red letters within three crosses that extend through all the lines of the poem. 
The letters within each cross spell out a verse from the [[New Testament]]:
*left: [[Luke 23]]:42: "'''Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom.'''"  
*middle: [[Matthew 27]]:46: "'''O God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me'''?" 
*right: [[Luke 23]]:39: "'''If thou art the Christ, save thyself and us.'''"
The "[[INRI]]" at the top of the middle cross stands for {{Lang|la|Iēsus Nazarēnus}}'','' {{Lang|la|Rēx Iūdaeōrum}}, Latin for "Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews" ([[wikisource:Bible (American Standard)/John#19:19|John 19:19]]). The three quotes represent the three figures crucified on Golgotha, as recorded in the gospels of Matthew and Luke.

(The text of the document shown differs significantly from the text usually published, including in the reference.<ref name="WilliamBrowne" /> Many of the lines have somewhat different wording; and while the acrostics are the same as far as they go, the published text is missing the last four lines, truncating the acrostics to "Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kin", "O God, my God, why hast thou forsak", and "If thou art the Christ, save thyself". The manuscript text is printed below, first as normal poetry, then spaced and bolded to bring out the acrostics. The word "Thou" in line 8 is not visible in this photograph, but is in the published version and is included in a cross-stitch sampler of the poem from 1793.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Legg |first=Sarah |date=April 2014 |title=a piece of antiquity: on the crucifixion of our saviour and the two thieves |url=https://i.imgur.com/kur6Oeg.jpg |access-date=24 May 2014}}{{better source needed|date=July 2024}}</ref>)

<poem style="margin-left: 2em">
Behold, O God! In rivers of my tears
I come to thee! bow down thy blessed ears
To hear my Plaint; and let thine eyes which keep
Continual watch behold a Sinner weep:
Let not, O God my God my Sins, tho' great,
And numberless, between thy Mercy's-Seat
And my poor Soul have place; since we are taught,
''[Thou]'' Lord, remember'st thyne, if Thou art sought.
I come not, Lord, with any other merit
Than what I by my Saviour Christ inherit:
Be then his wounds my balm— his stripes my Bliss;
His thorns my crown; my death be blest in his.
And thou, my blest Redeemer, Saviour, God,
Quit my accounts, withhold thy vengeful rod!
O beg for me, my hopes on Thee are set;
And Christ forgive me, since thou'st paid my debt
The living font, the Life, the Way, I know,
And but to thee, O whither shall I go?
All other helps are vain: grant thine to me,
For in thy cross my saving health I see.
O hearken then, that I with faith implore,
Lest Sin and Death sink me to rise no more.
Lastly, O God, my course direct and guide,
In Death defend me, that I never slide;
And at Doomsday let me be rais'd again,
To live with thee sweet Jesus say, Amen.
</poem>
